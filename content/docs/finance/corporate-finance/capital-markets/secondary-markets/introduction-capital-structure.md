## Introduction: The Great Financing Question

Every business needs money to operate and grow. But where should that money come from?

When Mukesh Ambani built Jio, Reliance invested over ₹2 lakh crore. Was it all equity? All debt? A mix? These choices—collectively called capital structure decisions—profoundly impact a company's risk, returns, and ultimate success.

Capital structure is the art and science of finding the right mix of debt and equity to fund a business.

---

## What is Capital Structure?

### Definition

Capital structure refers to the mix of debt and equity that a company uses to finance its assets and operations.

### The Basic Components

**1. Equity Capital**
- Share capital (money invested by shareholders)
- Retained earnings (profits kept in business)
- No fixed repayment obligation
- Shareholders are owners

**2. Debt Capital**
- Loans from banks/financial institutions
- Bonds/debentures
- Fixed interest and repayment obligations
- Lenders are creditors

### Visual Representation

```
Total Capital
├── Equity (Owners' Funds)
│   ├── Share Capital

    ├── Long-term Debt
    │   ├── Term Loans
    │   └── Debentures/Bonds
    └── Short-term Debt
        └── Working Capital Loans
```

## Debt vs Equity: The Fundamental Trade-off

### Characteristics Comparison

| Aspect | Debt | Equity |
|--------|------|--------|
| Ownership | No ownership rights | Ownership stake |
| Returns | Fixed interest | Variable dividends |
| Payment Priority | First claim | Residual claim |
| Tax Treatment | Interest is tax-deductible | Dividends not deductible |
| Risk to Company | Higher (must pay) | Lower (flexible) |
| Control | No voting rights | Voting rights |
| Maturity | Fixed repayment date | Permanent capital |
| Cost | Generally lower | Generally higher |

### Why Debt is "Cheaper"

**1. Tax Shield**
Interest payments are tax-deductible.

**Example:**
Company earns ₹100 lakh, pays ₹20 lakh interest, tax rate 25%

Without debt: Tax = 25% × ₹100L = ₹25 lakh
With debt: Tax = 25% × ₹80L = ₹20 lakh
**Tax saving: ₹5 lakh** (the tax shield)

**2. Lower Required Return**
Debt holders take less risk (priority in payment), so they demand lower returns.

Typical: Debt costs 10-12%, Equity expects 15-18%

## Measuring Capital Structure

### Debt-to-Equity Ratio

$$D/E\ Ratio = \frac{Total\ Debt}{Shareholders'\ Equity}$$

**Example:**
Debt: ₹60 crore, Equity: ₹40 crore
D/E = 60/40 = 1.5

**Interpretation:** For every ₹1 of equity, company has ₹1.50 of debt.

### Debt Ratio

$$Debt\ Ratio = \frac{Total\ Debt}{Total\ Assets}$$

**Example:**
Total Assets: ₹100 crore, Debt: ₹60 crore
Debt Ratio = 60/100 = 60%

### Interest Coverage Ratio

$$Interest\ Coverage = \frac{EBIT}{Interest\ Expense}$$

**Example:**
EBIT: ₹25 crore, Interest: ₹5 crore
Coverage = 25/5 = 5x

**Interpretation:** Company earns 5 times its interest obligation—comfortable position.

## Factors Affecting Capital Structure

### 1. Business Risk

**Higher business risk → Lower debt**

Business risk = variability in operating income

**High Business Risk Industries:**
- Technology (rapid change)
- Airlines (fuel, competition)
- Fashion retail (trend-dependent)

**Low Business Risk Industries:**
- Utilities (stable demand)
- FMCG (defensive)
- Pharma (inelastic demand)

### 2. Tax Position

**Higher tax rate → More debt attractive**

Tax shield value = Interest × Tax Rate

If company has tax losses, tax shield has no immediate value.

### 3. Asset Tangibility

**More tangible assets → More debt capacity**

Tangible assets (land, buildings, machinery) can be pledged as collateral, making lenders comfortable with more lending.

**High Tangibility:** Manufacturing, Real Estate
**Low Tangibility:** IT Services, Consulting

### 4. Growth Opportunities

**Higher growth → More equity preferred**

Growing companies:
- Need financial flexibility
- May have volatile cash flows
- Don't want debt constraints limiting investment

**Example:** Tech startups raise equity, not debt

### 5. Profitability

**Higher profitability → Less external capital needed**

Profitable companies generate internal funds (retained earnings), reducing need for external debt or equity.

**Paradox:** Many profitable companies have low debt because they don't need it.

### 6. Size

**Larger companies → More debt capacity**

Large companies:
- Diversified operations (lower risk)
- Better access to debt markets
- Lower borrowing costs
- More stable cash flows

### 7. Management Preferences

**Conservative management → Less debt**

Some promoters avoid debt to:
- Maintain control (equity dilution alternative is worse)
- Avoid financial stress
- Keep flexibility for opportunities

### 8. Market Conditions

**Favorable equity markets → More equity issuance**
**Low interest rates → More debt**

Companies time their capital raising based on market conditions.

## The Modigliani-Miller Propositions

### MM Proposition I (No Taxes)

In a perfect market (no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, no transaction costs):

**The value of a firm is independent of its capital structure.**

$$V_L = V_U$$

Where:
- V_L = Value of levered firm
- V_U = Value of unlevered firm

**Implication:** Capital structure doesn't matter—financing is just slicing the pie differently.

### MM Proposition II (No Taxes)

**Cost of equity increases with leverage:**

$$r_E = r_A + (r_A - r_D) \times \frac{D}{E}$$

Where:
- r_E = Cost of equity
- r_A = Cost of assets (unlevered)
- r_D = Cost of debt
- D/E = Debt-equity ratio

**Implication:** Even though debt is cheaper, the increased risk to equity holders raises equity cost, offsetting the benefit.

### MM with Taxes

When taxes exist:

$$V_L = V_U + (T_c \times D)$$

**Implication:** Debt creates value through tax shields. More debt = more value!

**But this can't be right—why don't all firms use 100% debt?**

## Why Not 100% Debt?

### Costs of Financial Distress

As debt increases, probability of financial distress rises.

**Financial Distress Costs:**
1. **Direct costs:** Legal fees, administrative costs of bankruptcy
2. **Indirect costs:**
   - Lost customers (will they honor warranties?)
   - Lost suppliers (will they extend credit?)
   - Lost employees (key talent leaves)
   - Management distraction
   - Fire sale of assets

### Agency Costs

**Conflict between shareholders and bondholders:**

1. **Asset substitution:** Shareholders may gamble with bondholder money
2. **Underinvestment:** Reject good projects if benefits go to bondholders
3. **Milking the property:** Take dividends, leaving less for bondholders

**Conflict between managers and shareholders:**

1. **Empire building:** Managers may overinvest using debt
2. **Risk aversion:** Managers may reject risky but valuable projects

### Trade-off Theory

**Optimal capital structure balances:**

Benefits of debt:
- Tax shields
- Discipline on managers

Costs of debt:
- Financial distress costs
- Agency costs

$$Optimal\ D/E = Balance\ where\ marginal\ benefit = marginal\ cost$$

## Pecking Order Theory

### The Hierarchy

Companies prefer financing sources in this order:

1. **Internal funds (retained earnings)** – First choice
2. **Debt** – Second choice
3. **Equity** – Last resort

### Why This Order?

**Information asymmetry:** Managers know more than outside investors.

- Issuing equity signals "our stock is overpriced"
- Issuing debt signals confidence in future cash flows
- Using internal funds avoids sending any signal

**Practical implication:** Profitable companies have low debt because they can fund internally.

## Capital Structure in Practice: Indian Examples

### Case: Infosys (Zero Debt Policy)

**Capital Structure:** Almost 100% equity
**D/E Ratio:** Near 0

**Reasons:**
- IT services have low capital requirements
- High profitability generates internal funds
- No tangible assets to pledge
- Risk-averse culture
- Massive cash reserves

**Trade-off:** Foregoes tax shields but maintains complete financial flexibility.

### Case: Tata Motors (High Debt)

**Capital Structure:** Significant debt
**D/E Ratio:** ~1.2-1.5

**Reasons:**
- Capital-intensive manufacturing
- Large tangible asset base
- Acquisitions (JLR) required funding
- Tax benefits valuable

**Challenge:** Debt servicing pressure during downturns (COVID impact).

### Case: Reliance Industries (Strategic Leverage)

**Capital Structure:** Moderate debt
**D/E Ratio:** ~0.8

**Strategy:**
- Use debt for mega-projects (Jio, Retail)
- Deleverage through equity sales (Facebook, Google investments)
- Maintain flexibility

**Lesson:** Dynamic capital structure management based on opportunities.

## Practical Considerations for Indian Companies

### Access to Debt Markets

**Large Companies:**
- Bank loans, bonds, commercial paper
- International markets (ECBs, FCCBs)
- Competitive rates

**MSMEs:**
- Limited to bank loans
- Higher interest rates
- Collateral requirements
- Personal guarantees needed

### Promoter Perspective

Many Indian businesses are promoter-controlled. Capital structure affects:

- **Control:** Equity dilution reduces promoter stake
- **Personal guarantees:** Promoters often guarantee company debt
- **Dividend capacity:** High debt reduces dividend flexibility

**Common preference:** Debt over equity dilution (maintain control)

### Regulatory Considerations

- **Banking sector norms:** Capital adequacy requirements
- **Infrastructure companies:** Lender covenants on D/E ratios
- **NBFCs:** RBI guidelines on leverage

## Key Takeaways

1. **Capital structure = Mix of debt and equity** – A fundamental corporate finance decision
2. **Debt is cheaper but risky** – Tax benefits vs. financial distress costs
3. **No universal optimal ratio** – Depends on industry, risk, assets, and strategy
4. **Trade-off theory:** Balance tax benefits against distress costs
5. **Pecking order:** Internal funds first, then debt, then equity
6. **Indian context:** Promoter control, bank-dominated lending, industry variations
7. **Dynamic management:** Capital structure should evolve with business needs

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the ideal debt-to-equity ratio?**
A: There's no universal ideal. It depends on industry (capital intensity), profitability, asset tangibility, growth stage, and risk appetite. Compare with industry peers.

**Q: Is debt always bad?**
A: No. Appropriate debt provides tax benefits and financial leverage. The key is sustainable debt—manageable interest coverage and repayment capacity.

**Q: Why do IT companies have almost no debt?**
A: Low capital requirements, high profitability, minimal tangible assets, and no need for external funding. They generate enough cash internally.

**Q: How does capital structure affect stock price?**
A: Optimal capital structure maximizes firm value and thus stock price. Too little debt foregoes tax benefits; too much debt increases distress risk.

**Q: Should startups use debt or equity?**
A: Usually equity. Startups have negative cash flows, no collateral, and uncertain futures—banks won't lend. Venture capital (equity) is the typical source.

*Capital structure is like choosing the right gear mix in a car. Too much debt (high gear) can make you fast but vulnerable on rough roads. Too much equity (low gear) is safe but inefficient. The art is finding the right balance for your specific journey—your industry, risk profile, and growth ambitions.*
